County-level migration analysis · Texas, 2010–2023

Is El Paso losing its next generation?

Six major Texas counties, one clear outlier. El Paso's 18–24 population is shrinking while every peer county is growing — but the reason isn't that young El Pasoans are fleeing in droves. It's that almost nobody new is showing up to replace them.

01 — the population itself

Every county grew. Except one.

Comparing 2013 to 2023, the 18–24 population in El Paso County fell 6.7% — the only decline among six major Texas counties. Tarrant County, by contrast, grew nearly 15% over the same stretch.

El Paso's own 25–30 cohort grew +11.3% over the same period — right in line with every other county. This isn't a story about El Paso losing population generally. It's specific to the youngest adults.
02 — the mechanism

It's not the exits. It's the front door.

Census's Migration Flows data lets us check the actual number of young people moving in and out of each county. El Paso's out-migration rate isn't unusual — it's the inflow that's strikingly low. For every 2 young adults who leave El Paso, roughly 1 arrives to take their place. Everywhere else, that ratio is much closer to even.

Put on a population-adjusted basis, the gap is stark: El Paso's 18-24 in-migration rate — new arrivals as a share of the existing cohort — is dead last of all six counties, at just 3.5%. The next-lowest county, Harris, is still 5.8%. Travis (Austin) pulls in nearly 14% — almost 4 times El Paso's rate. El Paso isn't slightly behind here. It's in a different tier entirely.

Narrow to just 18-24-year-olds and it's the worst of the six, by a wide margin. The problem is entirely concentrated in the youngest cohort — it disappears the moment you zoom out to everyone.

03 — where they go

Not far, mostly.

Combining every year of county-to-county flow data (2010–2020), Doña Ana County, New Mexico — home to Las Cruces, just across the state line — is by far El Paso's top destination. This is less "brain drain to another city" and more the two halves of one tightly linked border region.

04 — six hypotheses, tested against data

The badge and the uniform are doing real work.

Six hypotheses for El Paso's 25-30 pull were tested directly against microdata — not just argued for. Here's the scorecard, then the detail behind it.

This elevation is actually stronger among El Paso's 18-24 movers (24.5% federal, 18.8% active-duty) than its 25-30 movers — so this isn't a mechanism specific to the older cohort. Whoever does move to El Paso, at any age, skews heavily federal/military. Combined with the UTEP finding below: El Paso doesn't pull in many civilian 18-24 arrivals at all, so the few who do arrive are disproportionately there for military accession or a federal hiring pipeline, not a broader "young people are choosing El Paso" story.

The uniform pays the bills. It doesn't pay well.

Here's the wrinkle that complicates a purely rosy read of the government/military finding: El Paso's 25-30 arrivals have the lowest average income of all six counties — about 31% below the peer average ($25,727 vs. a $37,308 average elsewhere). That's not a small gap, and it's worth sitting with rather than glossing over.

A few ways to read it, and they're not mutually exclusive:

  • •Fort Bliss and CBP hire at ranks/grades that simply don't pay what a private-sector job in Austin or Dallas does at the same age — the pull here is job security and a guaranteed reason to relocate, not compensation.
  • •El Paso's overall wage levels and cost of living are lower than every peer metro in this comparison — some of this gap may simply reflect the regional economy, not something distinctive about who moves there.
  • •Enlisted military pay in particular is standardized nationally and doesn't scale with local cost of living the way a civilian salary would — a soldier stationed at Fort Bliss earns the same base pay they would anywhere else, while facing markedly lower housing costs. That can make the relative deal better than the raw income number suggests.

Put together: El Paso isn't winning the competition for young talent on pay. It's winning a narrower, structural competition — for the specific slice of 25-30-year-olds whose next move is dictated by a duty station or a federal hiring pipeline rather than a job offer they're choosing between several cities for. That's a real pull, but it's a different, more fragile kind of pull than "people are choosing El Paso" — it's closer to "people are being assigned to El Paso."

04b — testing the boomerang idea properly

Do people come home once they're established?

The "boomerang migration" idea is usually broader than one age band — that people leave young and return once settled, in their 40s, 50s, retirement. That's testable directly: extend the same Texas-born-share metric across six full age bands, not just 25-30.

Every other county levels off or ticks back up by 65+ — Dallas actually rises. El Paso is the only one that keeps declining all the way down, ending at 22.6% — the lowest value any county hits at any age. This directly answers the original question, and not the way the theory predicted: El Paso's oldest arrivals are the least likely to be Texas natives of any age group, in any of the six counties.

One coherent explanation ties this to the government/military finding above: it's consistent with military retirees of any origin choosing to settle near their last duty station after service, rather than native Texans coming home.

What about the Hispanic-specific version of this idea? Splitting El Paso's movers by Hispanic origin shows the identical pattern for Hispanic movers specifically: 54.4% Texas-born at 18-24, declining to 23.5% by 65+, no rebound. Hispanic movers are consistently more Texas-born than non-Hispanic movers at every age — unsurprising given regional demographics — but that gap doesn't widen with age the way a distinctly cultural "return to roots" pattern would predict. The data doesn't support a Hispanic-specific boomerang effect either.

This comparison rests on a much smaller non-Hispanic sample in El Paso (roughly 1 non-Hispanic mover per 12-19 Hispanic movers) — treat the non-Hispanic side as a rougher estimate than the Hispanic side.

05 — a testable reason why

El Paso's biggest university doesn't import 18-year-olds.

This is the one piece of the story that isn't inferred from migration numbers — it's an independently documented fact about UTEP, El Paso's largest university.

Every other county here has a university system that actively recruits 18-year-olds from elsewhere — UT-Austin, Texas A&M and UT-Arlington for Dallas-Fort Worth, UTSA, University of Houston. Freshman enrollment is a real driver of 18-24 in-migration in those metros. UTEP mostly isn't providing that pull — it's overwhelmingly educating people who were already there.

Source: College Factual and CollegeSimply, UTEP student demographics, 2025. This doesn't explain the full gap on its own — entry-level job market composition and general 18-24 lifestyle draw plausibly matter too — but it's the one piece of this narrative grounded in an external fact rather than inference from the migration numbers alone.

05b — checked against 63 other US counties

Does this hold up nationally?

Everything so far compares El Paso against five other Texas metros. To check whether the pattern is actually distinctive — or just how mid-sized border/Sun Belt counties look in general — the same analysis was extended to 64 counties nationally: the original six, 16 US-Mexico border counties, and the principal county of each of the top-50 US cities by population.

Young El Pasoans aren't leaving at an unusually high rate — if anything the opposite is true nationally, not just among Texas peers. But paired with the weak inflow, this is a low-mobility signature, not a healthy one: El Paso keeps company with the 14 places that have it worse — and that company is not encouraging.

One unplanned confirmation of the government/military mechanism: Cochise County, AZ — home to Fort Huachuca (Army Intelligence) — has the 3rd-highest out-migration rate of all 64 counties, despite being small, rural, and nothing like the big coastal metros that otherwise top this list. A population shaped by military rotation shows dramatically elevated churn — the same mechanism identified in El Paso, showing up independently in a completely different place.

The 14 places with a worse 18-24 in/out ratio than El Paso.

This is the list that should give pause. Every single county with a worse ratio than El Paso's 0.51 is either a small, economically struggling South Texas border county, or a Rust Belt city in long-term, well-documented population decline. There is no third category. El Paso is not in the company of dynamic, growing peer cities here — it's one step above places widely understood to be in genuine distress.

The difference between these three places tracks exactly what the UTEP hypothesis predicts: NMSU and the University of Arizona are real, out-of-state-recruiting universities with genuine college-town pull. UTEP is a 98% commuter school. Same border region, same general economic context — and the one place without a recruiting university shows a completely different migration signature. This is about as close to a controlled comparison as observational data gets.

The best 18-24 ratios nationally belong to two recognizable groups: genuine college towns with real outside draw (Tucson 1.10, Philadelphia 1.08) and major hub cities young people move to for jobs and culture (New York 1.42, San Francisco 1.34, Boston 1.26, Washington DC 1.03).

Methodology note: this national comparison uses county FIPS codes exclusively, not PUMA codes — a deliberate choice to avoid the PUMA-vintage risk that caused most of the earlier bugs in this project. New York City is represented by New York County (Manhattan) only, not all five boroughs; a few top-50 cities sharing a county (Phoenix/Mesa, Fort Worth/Arlington) are counted once.

06 — how this was built

Two Census products, cross-checked.

Population counts come from ACS 1-Year PUMS microdata. Migration counts and destinations come from Census's Migration Flows API — a pre-aggregated product where the Bureau has already done the geographic crosswalk, rather than attempting to rebuild it from raw microdata. Every county-code crosswalk used here was independently verified against Census's own place names, after an earlier pass turned out to have two counties silently mislabeled.

Full methodology, data limitations, and reproduction steps are in README.md alongside this file. Key limits worth knowing: county-to-county migration detail stops after the 2016–2020 vintage (a Census product change, not a gap in this analysis); age-cohort detail exists for only two vintages (2006–2010, 2011–2015); the replacement state-to-county product only tracks inbound moves, not outbound, so it can't extend the out-migration comparison past 2020.
